\subsection{Environmental Relations~\index{environmental relations}}

The anthropological study of human-environment relations~\index{human-environment relations} takes on new urgency in the face of climate change and ecological crisis. ECC provides novel perspective on how different societies establish and maintain patterns of coherence~\index{environmental relations!coherence patterns in} with their environments~\cite{ingold2000perception, ingold2011being}. Rather than choosing between materialist and symbolic approaches to environmental understanding, the framework suggests how ecological knowledge~\index{environmental knowledge} emerges from sustained patterns of energetic coherence developed through practical engagement~\index{environmental knowledge!integration with practice} with environments.

This approach illuminates traditional ecological knowledge~\index{traditional ecological knowledge} systems~\cite{berkes2012sacred, nadasdy2003hunters} in a new light. Instead of treating these as either primitive precursors to scientific understanding or purely cultural constructions, ECC reveals how they represent sophisticated patterns of coherence~\index{traditional ecological knowledge!coherence patterns in} developed through generations of careful observation and practice~\index{traditional ecological knowledge!integration with practice}. This perspective explains both their remarkable accuracy in managing environmental relationships~\index{traditional ecological knowledge!environmental management through} and their resistance to reduction to either technical knowledge or cultural belief.

Building on this foundation, the framework particularly enriches what Ingold~\cite{ingold2000perception, ingold2013making} termed the \textit{dwelling perspective}\index{environmental relations!dwelling perspective on} - how environmental understanding emerges from practical engagement rather than abstract observation. Through ECC, we can understand how different societies develop distinct but equally valid patterns of coherence for relating to their environments~\index{human-environment relations!integration of practice and meaning}, explaining both the stability of certain environmental relationships and their capacity for transformation through changes in practice~\index{environmental relations!transformation through practice}.

Such insights prove especially valuable for addressing contemporary environmental challenges~\cite{tsing2015mushroom, palsson2015nature}. Rather than viewing environmental problems as either purely technical issues or matters of cultural values alone, ECC suggests they emerge from disrupted patterns of coherence between human systems and environmental processes~\index{human-environment relations!coherence patterns in}. This explains why purely technical or purely cultural solutions often prove inadequate while pointing toward more integrated approaches.

Within this framework, the analysis of how societies transform nature through labor while maintaining specific ideological frameworks gains renewed relevance~\cite{bateson1972steps, bateson1979mind}. ECC demonstrates how patterns of energetic coherence integrate practical activity with cultural understanding~\index{environmental relations!integration of practice and understanding}, thereby explaining both the stability of certain technological-ideological configurations and the possibility for transformation through changing practices.

Similarly, the relationship between environmental knowledge and social power~\index{environmental knowledge!relationship to power} takes on new significance through this lens~\cite{tsing2015mushroom, povinelli2002cunning}. As societies develop distinct patterns of coherence for understanding and managing environmental relationships~\index{environmental knowledge!coherence patterns in}, these patterns reflect specific ways of organizing experience and action that prove more or less adaptive under particular conditions~\index{environmental knowledge!adaptive strategies in} - rather than representing either primitive wisdom or cultural limitation.

This perspective particularly enhances what recent scholarship has called \textit{more than human}\index{environmental relations!more than human perspectives} anthropology~\cite{kohn2013how, haraway2003companion}. Moving beyond treating human-environment relations~\index{human-environment relations} as either purely material or purely symbolic, ECC reveals how patterns of energetic coherence necessarily span human and non-human domains~\index{human-environment interaction}, explaining both the stability of certain environmental relationships and pathways for their transformation.

The framework also offers fresh insights into how different societies maintain what Rappaport~\cite{rappaport1984pigs, rappaport1999ritual} identified as ritual regulation of environmental relations~\index{environmental relations!ritual regulation of}. Through ECC, we can understand ritual practices as establishing patterns of coherence that enable effective environmental management~\index{traditional ecological knowledge!ritual regulation in} without requiring explicit ecological understanding - accounting for both the remarkable stability of traditional environmental practices and their capacity for adaptation.

Bateson's influential \textit{Steps to an ecology of mind}~\cite{bateson1972steps, bateson1979mind} similarly benefits from ECC's framework. His understanding of mind as inherently ecological~\index{ecological mind} aligns with ECC's emphasis on conscious states emerging from broader fields of energetic coherence. However, where earlier approaches sometimes risked losing specificity in broad cybernetic analogies, ECC grounds these insights in specific patterns of neural organization~\index{ecological mind!grounding in neural organization}.

Extending this analysis, work on different ontological schemas~\index{ontological schemas} - animism, naturalism, and analogism - can be interpreted as documenting distinct ways that human neural systems maintain coherent patterns of understanding across domains of experience~\index{ontological schemas!coherence patterns in}. Rather than viewing these as arbitrary cultural constructions, ECC suggests they represent sophisticated elaborations of basic patterns of energetic coherence shaped by both environmental engagement~\index{ontological schemas!environmental relations in} and social practice~\index{ontological schemas!integration with practice}.

These theoretical advances have practical implications for current debates about the Anthropocene and human modification of environmental systems~\cite{haraway2016staying, tsing2005friction}. Instead of positioning human cultural activity as inherently opposed to natural processes, ECC demonstrates how different patterns of energetic coherence enable different forms of environmental relationship~\index{human-environment relations!ecological knowledge in} - explaining both the persistence of destructive patterns and the possibility of alternative human-environment relationships.

A compelling example emerges in how indigenous movements for environmental justice establish new patterns of coherence between traditional ecological knowledge~\index{traditional ecological knowledge!sophistication of systems} and contemporary political action~\cite{nadasdy2007gift, nadasdy2003hunters}. Through the ECC framework, we can understand these movements as working not merely through protest or legal action but by maintaining and transforming sophisticated patterns of human-environment relationship~\index{human-environment relations!traditional knowledge systems}, accounting for both their effectiveness in specific struggles and their broader significance.

This perspective also transforms our understanding of environmental adaptation~\cite{strathern1980nature, strathern2004partial}. Moving beyond treating adaptation as either purely biological or purely cultural, ECC reveals how societies develop integrated patterns of coherence across multiple dimensions of environmental relationship~\index{environmental relations!integration of practice and understanding}. This holistic view explains both the remarkable stability of certain adaptive strategies and their capacity for transformation under changing conditions.

Moreover, the relationship between local and global environmental understanding~\index{environmental knowledge!local and global scales} gains clarity through ECC~\cite{tsing2015mushroom, latour2005reassembling}. Different scales of environmental relationship establish distinct but interrelated patterns of coherence, explaining why local environmental knowledge often proves more sophisticated than initially apparent to outside observers and how it might inform responses to global environmental challenges.

Taken together, these insights suggest new approaches to understanding both traditional environmental practices and emerging forms of ecological relationship~\cite{latour2004politics, wolf1999envisioning}. Rather than positioning these as opposing paradigms, ECC reveals how different traditions represent distinct but potentially complementary patterns of coherence~\index{environmental knowledge!indigenous systems of}. This framework offers ways to appreciate both the remarkable achievements of traditional ecological knowledge and the possibilities for developing new environmental relationships appropriate to contemporary challenges.
